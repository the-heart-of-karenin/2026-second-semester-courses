in the statement of the theorem by Sn \ p, where p is the north pole of Sn.
We then have C a homeomorphic image of Sn−1 with C ⊂Sn \ p. Now, by
the preceding theorem, Sn \ C has two components W1 and W2. Renaming
the components if necessary, we suppose that p ∈W1. By the result of Step 1,
W1 \ p is connected. Then W1 \ p and W2 are the components of (Sn \ p) \ C
and C equals the boundary of W2 and W1.
Theorem 4.9.7 (Invariance of domain) Let U be an open set in Rn and
let f : U →Rn be continuous and one-to-one. Then f(U) is open in Rn and
f : U →f(U) is a homeomorphism.
Proof: Without loss of generality, we can replace Rn by Sn.
Step 1. Given a point y of f(U), we show that f(U) contains a neighborhood
of y. This will prove that f(U) is open in Sn.
Let x be the point of U such that f(x) = y. Choose an open ball B(x, ϵ)
centered at x with radius ϵ > 0 chosen small enough that the closure of the ball,
B(x, ϵ), lies in U. Set S(x, ϵ) = B(x, ϵ)\B(x, ϵ). See Figure 4.9.5. Now the set
f(S(x, ϵ)) is homeomorphic to Sn−1. By Theorem 4.9.5, f(S(x, ϵ)) separates
Sn into two components W1 and W2, each open in Sn. The set f(B(x, ϵ)) is
connected and disjoint from f(S(x, ϵ)). Therefore, it lies in either W1 or W2.
Renaming if necessary, we may assume it lies in W1. Now it must equal all of
W1, for otherwise the set
Sn \ [f(B(x, ϵ)) ∪f(S(x, ϵ))] = Sn \ f(B(x, ϵ))
would consist of all of W2 along with points of W1 as well, contradicting
Theorem 4.9.3 which tells us that the cell f(B(x, ϵ)) does not separate Sn.
Therefore, f(B(x, ϵ)) = W1. We conclude that f(U) contains the neighbor-
hood W1 of y, as desired.
Step 2. In view of Step 1, f carries any set open in U to a set that is open in
Sn and hence open in f(U). Thus f is a homeomorphism of U with f(U).
