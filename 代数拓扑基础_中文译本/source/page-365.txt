In the case of a compact orientable manifold, it had been known for some
time that its homology had a ring structure. The multiplication in this ring
was called the intersection product, and it has a clear geometric meaning. But
all attempts to generalize this multiplication to more general spaces failed. It
was not until 1942, when Lefschetz gave a new definition of the multiplication
operation in cohomology, that it became clear why, for general spaces, there
exists a cohomology ring, but not a homology ring. It also became clear, about
the same time, what the relationship was between the homology and coho-
mology rings when both were defined: The Poincar´e duality theorem showed
that the two rings were isomorphic. We shall return to these matters later in
Sections 7.11 and 8.9.
Review of Rings, Modules, and Fields
We begin by reviewing some basic facts from algebra concerning rings and
modules.
A ring R is an abelian group, written additively, with a multiplication
operation, denoted · , satisfying two axioms:
(1) Associativity α · (β · γ) = (α · β) · γ.
(2) Distributivity
α · (β + γ) = α · β + α · γ,
(α + β) · γ = α · γ + β · γ.
[Absent parentheses, multiplications are performed before addi-
tions.]
If α · β = β · α for all α, β ∈R, then R is said to be commutative. If there is
an element 1 in R such that α · 1 = 1 · α = α for all α ∈R, then 1 is called a
unity element in R. If R has a unity element, then this element is easily seen
to be unique. Furthermore, one has (−1) · α = −α and 0 · α = 0 for all α ∈R.
If R is a commutative ring with unity, and if R satisfies the additional
condition that for every α̸ = 0, there is a β such that α · β = 1, then R is
called a field.
