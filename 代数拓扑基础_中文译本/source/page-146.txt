The separation axioms are not necessarily valid for quotient spaces. For
example, a quotient space of a Hausdorff space need not be Hausdorff. All that
one can say in general is the following: If p : X →Y is a quotient map, and
if each p−1(y) is closed in X, then Y is T1. That is to say, if X is Hausdorff,
then one-point sets in Y are closed. So if X∗is a partition of X into closed
sets, then the quotient space X∗is a T1-space.
We will treat separation axioms for quotient spaces in more detail later in
the book. We now treat one final question: Under what circumstances is the
cartesian product of two quotient maps a quotient map? We have two results
that address this question.
Theorem 2.8.2 Let p : X →Y be a quotient map. If C is a locally compact
Hausdorff space, then
p × iC : X × C →Y × C
is a quotient map.
Proof: Let π = p × iC. Let A be a subset of Y × C such that π−1(A) is open
in X × C. We show that A is open in Y × C. That is, given (y0, c0) ∈A, we
will find an open set containing (y0, c0) and lying in A. Choose x0 ∈X so that
p(x0) = y0. Then π(x0, c0) = (y0, c0). Since π−1(A) is open, we can choose
neighborhoods U1 of x0 and W of c0 such that U1 × W ⊂π−1(A). Because C
is locally compact Hausdorff, we can choose a neighborhood V of c0 so that
V is compact and V ⊂W. Then U1 × V is a neighborhood of (x0, c0) such
that V is compact and
U1 × V ⊂π−1(A) .
In general, suppose that Ui is a neighborhood of x0 such that Ui × V ⊂
π−1(A). Now p−1p(Ui) is not necessarily open in X, but it contains Ui. We
construct an open set Ui+1 ⊂X such that
p−1p(Ui) × V ⊂Ui+1 × V ⊂π−1(A)
as follows: For each point x of p−1p(Ui), the space {x} × V lies in π−1(A).
Using the compactness of V , we choose a neighborhood Wx of x such that
Wx × V ⊂π−1(A). Let Ui+1 be the union of the open sets Wx. Then Ui+1 is
the desired open subset of X. See Figure 2.8.2.
