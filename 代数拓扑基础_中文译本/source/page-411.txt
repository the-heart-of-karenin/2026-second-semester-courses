In differential geometry, it is common to deal with compact manifolds and
to use the field of reals as coefficients. Because the cohomology and homology
vector spaces are dual in this case, differential geometers sometimes treat
homology and cohomology as if they were the same object. Unfortunately,
this practice can lead to confusion.
The next definition is used in Exercise 4.
Definition 7.3.8 A homomorphism α : G →G′ gives rise to homomorphisms
α∗: Hi(C; G) →Hi(C; G′) ,
α∗: Hi(C; G) →Hi(C; G′) .
They are called coefficient homomorphisms.
EXERCISES
1. Let C be a free chain complex. Show that if Hp−1(C) is free or if G
is divisible [see Definition 5.2.9], then the Kronecker map κ is an
isomorphism.
2. Use the universal coefficient theorem to compute the cohomology
with general coefficients G of T # T, P2 # P2 # P2, PN, and the k-
fold dunce cap.
3. Assume Hi(X) is finitely generated for all i.
(a) Compute Hp(X; G) in terms of the betti numbers and torsion
coefficients of X. Compare with Exercise 5 of Section 5.3.
(b) Repeat (a) when G is the circle group S1 = R/Z.
(c) Repeat (a) when G = Q.
4. Show that the universal coefficient sequence for cohomology is nat-
ural with respect to coefficient homomorphisms.
5. Let (K, K0) be a relative pseudo n-manifold. [See Definition 5.4.5.]
Let K be finite. Show that the torsion subgroup of Hn−1(K, K0)
vanishes if (K, K0) is orientable, and has order 2 otherwise.
6. Explain why the splitting in Theorem 7.3.1 is not natural.
7. Explain why the splitting in Corollary 7.3.2 is not natural.
