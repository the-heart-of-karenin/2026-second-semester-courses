The equator in Sn is a homeomorphic copy of Sn−1. The two connected
components of its compliment are homeomorphic to open balls and their clo-
sures are n-cells. So under the hypotheses of the preceding theorem, one might
conjecture that if W1 and W2 are the components of Sn \ C, where C is an
n−1 sphere, then the sets W 1 and W 2 would be n-cells. This is true for n = 2,
and is known as Schoenflies Theorem [a proof may be found in [New51]], but
for n > 2 additional hypotheses are needed. The result for n ≥3 is known
as the Generalized Schoenflies Theorem. It was proved independently by M.
Brown and B. Mazur. The relevant papers are [Maz59], [Maz61], [Bro60], and
[Mor60].
The difficulty in dimensions 3 and higher was discovered by J. W. Alexan-
der in 1924 [in [Ale24]]. He constructed a surface that later came to be known
as the Alexander horned sphere. The construction starts by removing a section
from a torus as shown at the top of Figure 4.9.4. The two holes are patched
with discs, so that the surface is homeomorphic to a sphere. Alexander then
took two smaller partial tori and attached one to each disc by putting a tube
from a small hole in the disc to a hole in the small partial torus that is be-
ing added. This process was then repeated infinitely often. The lower part of
Figure 4.9.4 showing the smaller partial tori is from Alexander’s original pa-
