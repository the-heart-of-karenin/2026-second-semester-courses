If h : X →Y is a continuous map, then h∗is a ring homomorphism. There-
fore a homotopy equivalence induces a ring isomorphism. It follows that the
cohomology ring is a topological invariant, in fact, a homotopy-type invariant.
Commutativity
We have not yet discussed whether or not the cohomology ring is commuta-
tive. In fact it is not, in general. Instead, it has a property commonly called
anticommutativity. Specifically, if αp ∈Hp(X; R) and βq ∈Hq(X; R), then
αp ⌣βq = (−1)pq βq ⌣αp
holds. We shall not prove this formula now, for the proof will become much
easier when we give an alternative definition of the cup product later on.
In the next section, we shall compute the cohomology ring in several spe-
cific cases. But first, let us introduce several generalized versions of the cup
product.
Cup Products with General Coefficients
Let G be an arbitrary abelian group. Then we note that the cup product
formula makes sense if we interpret it as a function
Sp(X) × Sq(X; G) ⌣
−→Sp+q(X; G) .
In this case, cp is a cochain with integral values, cq is a cochain with values in
G, and the multiplication on the right-hand side of the formula (∗) is the usual
product operation sending (n, g) to ng. Bilinearity is immediate. Associativity
holds when it makes sense—that is, when it involves a map
Sp(X) × Sq(X) × Sr(X; G) ⌣
−→Sp+q+r(X; G) .
The cochain z0 whose value is 1 on each 0-simplex T acts as a left unity ele-
ment. The proof of the coboundary formula is unchanged, as is the proof that
the homomorphism h∗induced by a continuous map preserves cup products.
Therefore we have a well-defined cup product operation
Hp(X) × Hq(X; G) ⌣
−→Hp+q(X; G) .
The most general cup product operation usually considered begins with
a bilinear map α : G × G′ →G′′, called a “coefficient pairing.” Using this
map to replace the multiplication operation in (∗), one has a well-defined cup
product
Hp(X; G) × Hq(X; G′) ⌣
−→Hp+q(X; G′′) .
We shall not need this degree of generality.
