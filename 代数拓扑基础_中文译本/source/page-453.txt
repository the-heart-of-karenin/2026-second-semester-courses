Elements of Algebraic Topology
The similarity of the cochain cross product formula to the formula for the
cup product may make you suspect that there is some relation between the
two. That relation is described in the next theorem.
Theorem 7.11.4 If d : X →X × X is the diagonal map, given by d(x) =
(x, x), then
d∗(αp × βq) = αp ⌣βq
holds, for both versions of the cohomology cross product.
Proof: Let zp and zq be representative cocycles for αp and βq, respectively.
If T : ∆p+q →X is a singular (p + q)-simplex, then we compute
⟨d#(zp × zq), T⟩
= ⟨zp × zq, d ◦T⟩
= ⟨zp, π1 ◦(d ◦T) ◦ℓ(ϵ0, ϵ1, . . . , ϵp)⟩· ⟨zq, π2 ◦(d ◦T) ◦ℓ(ϵp, ϵp+1, . . . , ϵm)⟩
= ⟨zp ⌣zq, T⟩,
where we have used the fact that π1 ◦d = iX = π2 ◦d.
This last theorem is due to Lefschetz. It shows why cohomology has a ring
structure, but homology does not. Both homology and cohomology have cross
products, but only in cohomology can one form the composite of the cross
product with the homomorphism induced by the diagonal map. In homology
one has the diagram
Hp(X) ⊗Hq(X)
Hp+q(X × X)

d∗
and we see that the mapping d∗goes in the wrong direction!
Corollary 7.11.5 Cup products are anticommutative in that
αp ⌣βq = (−1)pgβq ⌣αp
holds, for both versions of the cup product.
Proof: Let d be the diagonal map as before, and let λ : X × X →X × X be
the map that reverses coordinates. Then since λ ◦d = d, we compute
αp ⌣βq = d∗(αp × βq) = (λ ◦d)∗(αp × βq)
= d∗((−1)pqβq ⌣αp) = (−1)pgβq ⌣αp .
Next we compute cup products in the cohomology of a product space.
Theorem 7.11.6 In the cohomology ring H∗(X × Y ; R), the following for-
mula holds:
(α × β) ⌣(α′ × β′) = (−1)pq(α ⌣α′) × (β ⌣β′) ,
where p = dim α′ and q = dim β.
