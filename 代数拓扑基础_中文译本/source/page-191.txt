We now have all the necessary pieces for a homology theory.
First, we note that the class of triangulable pairs forms an admissible class
of spaces for a homology theory. If (X, A) is triangulable, then so are (X, X),
(X, ∅), (A, A), and (A, ∅). Of course any one-point space is triangulable. Fi-
nally, if (X, A) is triangulable, then so is (X × I, A × I) by Lemma 2.7.3.
Theorem 3.6.2 Simplicial homology theory on the class of triangulable pairs
satisfies the Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms.
Proof: Axioms 1–5 and 7 express familiar properties of the homology of
simplicial complexes that carry over at once to the homology of triangulable
pairs. Only Axioms 6 and 8, the excision axiom and the compact support
axiom, require some thought.
To check the compact support axiom, it suffices to show that it holds for
simplicial complexes. Let α be an element of Hp(K, K0). Let c be a chain
of K representing α. Its boundary is carried by K0. Since c is carried by a
finite subcomplex L of K, it can be considered as a cycle of (L, L0), where
L0 = K0 ∩L. If β is the homology class of c in Hp(L, L0), then j∗(β) = α,
where j is inclusion of (L, L0) in (K, K0). Thus this axiom is verified.
Checking the excision axiom involves a subtlety that may not at first be
apparent. The problem is that even though both (X, A) and (X \ U, A \ U)
may be triangulable, the two triangularizations may be entirely unrelated to
one another. If this is not the case, then the excision axiom follows readily
from Theorem 1.10.6, as we now show.
