Chain-level proof: We assume that Sn is triangulated so that the antipodal
map a : Sn →Sn is simplicial, and that Pn is triangulated so that p : Sn →Pn
is simplicial. [Lemma 4.14.11 will show that this is possible. For now, we
assume it.]
We use simplicial homology. Let cn be a fundamental cycle for (En
+, Sn−1).
Then p#(cn) is a fundamental cycle for (Pn, Pn−1), by Lemma 4.13.3.
Consider the following chain of Sn:
γn = cn + (−1)n−1a#(cn) .
It is a cycle, for its boundary is
∂γn
=
∂cn + (−1)n−1a#(∂cn)
=
∂cn + (−1)2n−1∂cn = 0 .
This equation follows from the fact that a : Sn−1 →Sn−1 has degree (−1)n,
so that a#, mapping the (n −1)-cycle group of Sn−1 to itself, equals multipli-
cation by (−1)n. Furthermore, γn is a multiple of no other cycle of Sn, for its
restriction to En
+ is cn, which is a fundamental cycle for (En
+, Sn−1). Thus γn
is a fundamental cycle for Sn.
Finally, we compute
p#(γn) = p#(cn + (−1)n−1a#(cn)) .
Since p ◦a = p, we conclude that
p#(γn) = [1 + (−1)n−1]p#(cn) .
Since γn is a fundamental cycle for Sn and p#(cn) is a fundamental cycle for
(Pn, Pn−1), the lemma follows.
