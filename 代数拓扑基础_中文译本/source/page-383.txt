Later we will show how to compute tensor products. For the present, we

Theorem 6.2.4 There is an isomorphism
Z ⊗G ∼= G
that maps n ⊗g to ng. It is natural with respect to homomorphisms of G.
Proof: The function mapping Z × G to G that sends (n, g) to ng is bilinear,
so it induces a homomorphism ϕ : Z ⊗G →G sending n ⊗g to ng.
Let ψ : G →Z ⊗G be defined by the equation ψ(g) = 1 ⊗g. Then ψ is a
homomorphism. For g ∈G, we have
ϕψ(g) = ϕ(1 ⊗g) = g ,
while on a typical generator n ⊗g of Z ⊗G, we have
ψϕ(n ⊗g) = ψ(ng) = 1 ⊗(ng) = n ⊗g .
Thus ψ is an inverse for ϕ.

Before we derive some general properties of tensor products, let us first
note a common fallacy. Suppose A′ is a subgroup of A, and B′ is a subgroup of
B. Then it is tempting to assume that A′ ⊗B′ can be considered a subgroup
of A ⊗B, but this is not correct in general. While the inclusion mappings
i : A′ →A and j : B′ →B do give rise to a homomorphism
i ⊗j : A′ ⊗B′ →A ⊗B ,
this homomorphism is not necessarily injective. As an example, let A = Q, the
additive group of rational numbers, and let B = Z/2. Then A ⊗B = Q ⊗Z/2
is in fact trivial, because in Q ⊗Z/2 we have
a ⊗b = (a/2) ⊗2b = (a/2) ⊗0 = 0 .
For the subgroups, we let A′ = Z and B′ = B = Z/2. Then A′ ⊗B′ = Z⊗Z/2
which is non-trivial, so it cannot be a subgroup of the trivial group A ⊗B =
Q ⊗Z/2.
