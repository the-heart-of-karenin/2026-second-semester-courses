Elements of Algebraic Topology
Sometimes one begins with a space X and seeks to give it the structure
of a CW complex. This we did in Examples 4.12.4, 4.12.5, and 4.12.6. On
the other hand, sometimes one seeks to construct new spaces that are CW
complexes by the process of pasting balls together. This is what we did in
Example 4.12.13. The general construction is described in the two theorems
we just proved. This construction significantly enlarges the class of spaces
about which algebraic topology has something interesting to say.
EXERCISES
1. Let X be a CW complex, and let A be a compact subset of X.
(a) Show that A intersects only finitely many open cells of X.
Where do you use “closure-finiteness” in the proof?
(b) Show that A lies in a finite subcomplex of X.
2. Let X be a CW complex. Suppose h : |K| →X is a triangulation
of X that induces a triangulation of each skeleton Xp.
(a) Show that h induces a triangulation of each set eα.
(b) Show that if Kp is the subcomplex of K triangulating Xp, then
Kp has dimension at most p. [Hint: Use local homology groups.]
3. Let X be a CW complex. Show that the topology of X is compactly
generated. [See Exercise 3 of Section 4.11.]
4. Let X and Y be a CW complexes. Then X ×Y is the union of open
cells eα × eβ, for eα a cell of X and eβ a cell of Y .
(a) Show that if Y is locally compact, then X×Y is a CW complex.
(b) Show that X ×C Y is a CW complex in general. [See Exercise 3
of Section 4.11.]
5. Verify that a regular cell complex can be triangulated so that each
closed cell is the polytope of a subcomplex.
6. Show that the n-dimensional torus is a CW-complex.
7. Show that the solid torus in 3-dimensional space is a CW-complex.
