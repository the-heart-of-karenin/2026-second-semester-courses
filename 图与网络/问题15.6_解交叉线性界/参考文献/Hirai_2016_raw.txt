ON UNCROSSING GAMES FOR SKEW-SUPERMODULAR FUNCTIONS


                                               Hiroshi Hirai
                                          The University of Tokyo


                        (Received September 29, 2015; Revised December 12, 2015)

Abstract In this note, we consider the uncrossing game for a skew-supermodular function f , which is a two-
player game with players, Red and Blue, and abstracts the uncrossing procedure in the cut-covering linear
program associated with f . Extending the earlier results by Karzanov for {0, 1}-valued skew-supermodular
functions, we present an improved polynomial time strategy for Red to win, and give a strongly polynomial
time uncrossing procedure for dual solutions of the cut-covering LP as its consequence. We also mention its
implication on the optimality of laminar solutions.

    Keywords: Combinatorial optimization, uncrossing game, skew-supermodular function,
    cut-covering LP

1. Introduction
Let V be a finite set, and let S(V ) denote the set of all bi-partitions of V . A member
{X, V \ X} of S(V ) is also denoted by X or V \ X (if no confusion occurs). A pair X, Y
of members in S(V ) is said be crossing if X ∩ Y , V \ (X ∪ Y ), X \ Y , and Y \ X are all
nonempty. A family F ⊆ S(V ) is called laminar if there is no crossing pair in F. A function
f : S(V ) → R+ is called skew-supermodular if it satisfies

              f (X) + f (Y ) ≤ max{f (X ∩ Y ) + f (X ∪ Y ), f (X \ Y ) + f (Y \ X)}

for every pair X, Y ∈ S(V ). For a skew-supermodular function f , we consider the following
game involving two players Blue and Red, which we call the uncrossing game.
Uncrossing game for skew-supermodular function f
Input: A family F ⊆ S(V ).
Step 1: If F is laminar, then Red wins; the game terminates.
Step 2: Otherwise, Red chooses a crossing pair (X, Y ) in F, chooses (X ′ , Y ′ ) ∈ {(X ∩
    Y, X ∪ Y ), (X \ Y, Y \ X)} satisfying

                                      f (X) + f (Y ) ≤ f (X ′ ) + f (Y ′ ),

   and replaces X, Y by X ′ , Y ′ in F.
Step 3: Blue returns one of X, Y to F. Go to step 1.
Here we assume that Red has an evaluation oracle of f .
   The uncrossing game abstracts the uncrossing procedure in combinatorial optimization,
and was originally introduced by Hurkens, Lovász, Schrijver and Tardos [3]. The original
formulation is the following. For an input family F of subsets of V , if F is a chain, then
Red wins. Otherwise Red chooses an incomparable pair (X, Y ) in F and replaces (X, Y ) by
(X ∩ Y, X ∪ Y ) in F. Then Blue returns one of X and Y to F. Hurkens, Lovász, Schrijver
and Tardos showed that there is a strategy (i.e., a way of choosing (X, Y )) for Red to win

after a polynomial number O(|V ||F|) of iterations. Karzanov [5] considered a symmetric
generalization on cross-closed families. Here a family S ⊆ S(V ) is called cross-closed if for
X, Y ∈ S, X ∩ Y, X ∪ Y belong to S or X \ Y, Y \ X belong to S. In his uncrossing game,
the input is a family F ⊆ S, and Red chooses a crossing pair (X, Y ) in F and chooses
(X ′ , Y ′ ) ∈ {(X ∩ Y, X ∪ Y ), (X \ Y, Y \ X)} with X ′ , Y ′ ∈ S. As was noticed by Karzanov,
the original uncrossing game can be viewed as a special case of the cross-closed family on
V ∪ {s, t} consisting of X with |X ∪ {s, t}| = 1. He showed that there is a strategy for Red
to win after O(|V |4 |F|) iterations.
    A cross-closed family S is naturally identified with a {0, 1}-valued skew-supermodular
function f defined by f (X) := 1 for X ∈ S and f (X) := 0 otherwise. By this identification,
the uncrossing game on the cross-closed family S reduces to our setting. We do not know
whether the converse reduction is possible. Also Karzanov’s strategy [5] seems not to be
applied to our generalization. Indeed, his strategy includes selection rules of type “if X ̸∈ S,
then Red takes...”[5, p. 222]. The main aim of this note is to present an improved Red-win
strategy for our generalization.
Theorem 1. For every skew-supermodular function f and every input F, there exists a
strategy for Red to win after O(|V |3 |F|) iterations
    The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we prove Theorem 1. Our
strategy basically follows Karzanov’s one in high level, and incorporates nontrivial modifi-
cations in an essential part. In Section 3, we explain implications of this result on skew-
supermodular cut-covering linear programs, which constitute an important subclass of linear
programs appearing from a wide variety of network design problems in combinatorial opti-
mization. As was noted by [5] (for {0, 1}-skew-supermodular cases), the uncrossing game
naturally arises from the uncrossing procedure of dual solutions, and a Red-win strategy
gives rise to a strongly polynomial time uncrossing algorithm. Our strategy in Theorem 1 is
applicable to general skew-supermodular cut-covering linear programs beyond that treated
in [5]. We also present an unexpected consequence on the optimality property of laminar
dual solutions.

2. Proof
Let F be a set of bi-partitions on V . A member X ∈ F is said to be trivial (in F) if no
member of F is crossing with X. If Z is not crossing with X and Y , then it is not crossing
with X ∩ Y , X ∪ Y , X \ Y , and Y \ X. In particular, a trivial member remains trivial after
uncrossings. Therefore we can assume that a trivial member is removed from F whenever
it appears. Define an equivalence relation ∼F on V by i ∼F j if there is no X ∈ F with
|{i, j} ∩ X| = 1. An equivalence class of this relation is called an atom. If i ̸∼F j, then i and
j are said to be separated. Obviously each X ∈ F is a disjoint union of atoms. Therefore
we can identify the ground set V with the set of all atoms. Notice that i ∼{X∩Y,X∪Y } j
implies i ∼{X,Y } j, and i ∼{X\Y,Y \X} j implies i ∼{X,Y } j. Therefore, if F ′ is obtained from
F through iterations of the game, then ∼F ′ coarsens ∼F , i.e., i ∼F j implies i ∼F ′ j. By
this fact together with the removal of trivial members, it happens that several atoms in F
are joined to a single atom in F ′ . In this case, the cardinality of the ground set decreases.
     We first consider the essential case of input F to which the argument in [5] is not directly
applicable. Let V = {1, 2, . . . , n} (n ≥ 4). Suppose that F satisfies:
(A) F is the disjoint union of two subsets A and B such that every member in A takes
  form {1, 2, . . . , i} for some 2 ≤ i ≤ n − 2, and every member of B takes form {2, 3, . . . , j}
  for some 3 ≤ j ≤ n − 1.

In the following, we denote {i, i + 1, i + 2, . . . , j} by [i, j]. We may assume that B ̸= ∅. Let
d(≥ 3) be the minimum number for which [2, d] belongs to B. We can assume that [1, i]
belongs to A for i = 2, 3, . . . , d − 1; otherwise some i and i + 1 are not separated, and are
joined to a single element. We give a strategy for Red to keep the game of form (A) and to
decrease n + |B| after O(d) iterations.
     Initially, Red evaluates f ([1, d−1])+f ([2, d]), f ({1})+f ({d}), and f ([2, d−1])+f ([1, d]).
Suppose that f ([1, d−1])+f ([2, d]) ≤ f ({1})+f ({d}) holds. Red chooses X = [1, d−1], Y =
[2, d], chooses X ′ = X \ Y = {1}, and Y ′ = Y \ X = {d}, and replaces X, Y by X ′ , Y ′ ; both
X ′ , Y ′ are singletons (trivial) and vanish. If Blue returns X = [1, d − 1], then |B| decreases.
If Blue returns Y = [2, d], then |B| does not change, d and d − 1 are not separated, and
hence n decreases.
     Suppose that f ([1, d − 1]) + f ([2, d]) > f ({1}) + f ({d}) holds. By skew-supermodularity,
f ([1, d − 1]) + f ([2, d]) ≤ f ([2, d − 1]) + f ([1, d]) must hold. Suppose that d = 3. Red chooses
X = [1, 2], Y = [2, 3], X ′ = X ∪ Y = [1, 3], and Y ′ = X ∩ Y = {2}. Then {2} vanishes;
in particular |B| does not increase. If Blue returns X = [1, 2], then |B| decreases. If Blue
returns Y = [2, 3], then 2 and 3 are not separated, and n decreases.
     Suppose that d > 3. Red computes the smallest k ∈ [2, d − 1] such that
       f ([1, l]) + f ([2, l + 1]) ≤ f ([2, l]) + f ([1, l + 1]) (l = k, k + 1, . . . , d − 1).   (2.1)
Such an index k actually exists since f ([1, d−1])+f ([2, d]) ≤ f ([2, d−1])+f ([1, d]). If k > 2,
then it holds f ([1, k − 1]) + f ([2, k]) > f ([2, k − 1]) + f ([1, k]), and by skew-supermodularity,
it holds
                           f ([1, k − 1]) + f ([2, k]) ≤ f ({1}) + f ({k}).                    (2.2)
Also, in the case of k = 2, (2.2) holds since f ({1}) + f ({2}) ≤ f ({1}) + f ({2}). Adding
inequalities (2.1), we obtain
                             f ([1, k]) + f ([2, d]) ≤ f ([1, d]) + f ([2, k]).
Red chooses X = [1, k], Y = [2, d], X ′ = X ∪ Y = [1, d], and Y ′ = X ∩ Y = [2, k], and
replaces X, Y by X ′ , Y ′ . If Blue returns X = [1, k], then |B| does not change, d decreases,
and Red goes to the initial stage above. Suppose that Blue returns Y = [2, d]. If k = 2,
then Y ′ is a singleton and vanishes, and 2 and 3 are not separated; hence n + |B| decreases.
Suppose that k > 2. In the next iteration, |B| increases by one, and d becomes k. By (2.2),
Red chooses X = [1, k − 1], Y = [2, k], X ′ = X \ Y = {1}, and Y ′ = Y \ X = {k}. Both
X ′ and Y ′ are singletons and vanish in the next iteration. If Blue returns [1, k − 1], then B
decreases by one, k and k + 1 are not separated (since now [1, k] does not exist), and n + |B|
is smaller than that in two iterations before. If Blue returns [2, k], then k − 1 and k are not
separated, n + |B| is equal to that in two iterations before, d = k is smaller than before, and
Red goes to the initial stage above.
    Summarizing, by using the above strategy, Red can keep the game of form (A) and
decrease n + |B| after O(d) iterations. Thus Red wins after O(n2 ) iterations. We remark
that the above strategy is easily adapted for the case where Blue is allowed to return none
of X, Y .
    The rest of arguments is exactly the same as that given in [5], and is sketched as follows.
A subset X ⊆ V is said to be 2-partitioned for F if X intersects at most two atoms with
respect to F. Suppose that
(B) the input F is the disjoint union of two laminar families C and D such that for each
    X ∈ C, X or V \ X is 2-partitioned for D.

In this case, for any X ∈ C the family D ∪ {X} satisfies the condition (A) after removing
trivial members in D ∪ {X}. Indeed, D ∪ {X} consists of X and members Y1 , Y2 , . . . , Ym
in D crossing with X. We can assume that X is 2-partitioned for D. By this condition, X
is the disjoint union of Z1 and Z2 such that X ∩ Yi ∈ {Z1 , Z2 } for i = 1, 2, . . . , m. We can
assume that both Z1 and Z2 are nonempty, and Z2 = X ∩ Yi for each i (by Yi ↔ V \ Yi ).
By the laminarity of D together with ∅ =     ̸ Z1 ⊆ V \ (Yi ∪ Yj ) and ∅ ̸= Z2 ⊆ Yi ∩ Yj , we have
Yi ⊂ Yj or Yj ⊂ Yi for i ̸= j. By rearranging them, we have Z2 ⊂ Y1 ⊂ Y2 ⊂ · · · ⊂ Ym .
Atoms are Z1 , Z2 , Y1 \ Z2 , V \ (Ym ∪ Z1 ), and Yi+1 \ Yi for i = 1, 2, . . . , m − 1. Thus the
situation reduces to (A) in the setting of A = {[1, 2]}, B = {[2, j] | 3 ≤ j ≤ m + 2}, and
n = m + 3.
    The strategy for Red is the following. Red chooses maximal {X, V \ X} ∈ C in the
sense that X is 2-partitioned for D and there is no {Y, V \ Y } ∈ C such that X ⊂ Y
(proper inclusion) and Y is 2-partitioned for D. As above, the family D ∪ {X} satisfies
the condition (A) (after removing trivial members). Therefore Red plays the game within
D ∪ {X} and obtains a laminar family D′ by the above strategy after O(|V |2 ) iterations.
Since D′ is obtained from D ∪ {X} by uncrossings, ∼D′ coarsens ∼D∪{X} (see the beginning
of Section 2). By this fact together with the maximality of X and the laminarity of C, the
union of C ′ := C \ {X} and D′ satisfies the condition (B). Let C ← C ′ and D ← D′ . Red
repeats the same procedure for C and D, and wins after |C| steps. The total number of
iterations is O(|C||V |2 ) = O(|V |3 ); recall that the size of any laminar family on V is O(|V |).
    Suppose finally that F is arbitrary. Red chooses a (maximal) laminar subset C of F,
and chooses an arbitrary X ∈ B := F \ C. Then the union of C and D = {X} obviously
satisfies (B). Red applies the above strategy for C ∪ D, and obtains a laminar family C ′ after
O(|V |3 ) iterations. Let C ← C ′ and B ← B \{X}. Red repeats the same procedure for C and
B (until B = ∅), and wins after O(|F|) steps. The total number of iterations is O(|V |3 |F|),
as required.

3. Implications
As mentioned in Introduction, the uncrossing game abstracts the uncrossing procedure
arising from a class of cut-covering linear programs. Let G = (V, E) be an undirected graph
with edge-cost a : E → R+ , and let f : S(V ) → R+ be a skew-supermodular function.
∑ skew-supermodular cut-covering LP is the following linear program: Minimize the cost

and its variation capture a wide variety of network design problems and their fractional
relaxations. Examples include matching, T-join, network synthesis, survivable network,
traveling salesman, Steiner tree/forest, connectivity augmentation and so on; see, e.g., [4, 6].

played key roles in algorithm design and analysis: Edmonds’ blossom algorithm [1] for
weighted matching works with a laminar dual solution, which enables us to avoid keeping
exponential number of inequalities/variables for matching polytope. Jain’s iterative round-
ing algorithm [4] for survivable network was obtained by analyzing the larminarity property
of skew-supermodular covering LPs.
    The existence of a laminar dual solution can be seen from the following standard uncross-
ing argument. Let λ be an arbitrary feasible solution. Suppose that F(λ) is not laminar.
Choose a crossing pair (X, Y ) in F(λ), and choose (X ′ , Y ′ ) ∈ {(X ∩Y, X ∪Y ), (X \Y, Y \X)}
with f (X) + f (Y ) ≤ f (X ′ ) + f (Y ′ ). Decrease λ by α := min{λ(X), λ(Y )} on X and
Y , and increase λ by α on X ′ and Y ′ . The objective value does not decrease (by skew-
supermodularity) and the feasibility is also preserved. This operation is called an uncross-
ing. For rational λ, we obtain a laminar solution after a finite number of uncrossings, where
λ is said to be uncrossed.
    The uncrossing process gives rise to the uncrossing game with input F = F(λ), as
mentioned in [5] (under the setting of a cross-closed family). Red chooses a crossing pair
X, Y in F, and replaces X, Y by X ′ , Y ′ in F. Blue returns one X̃ of X, Y for λ(X̃) >
min{λ(X), λ(Y )}; Blue returns none of them if α = λ(X) = λ(Y ). ∑     Then F = F(λ) holds in
the next iteration. Suppose that λ is integer-valued. Observe that X∈S(V ) |X||V \ X|λ(X)
strictly decreases in one uncrossing. Therefore, in any choices of uncrossing pairs, this
process terminates after O(|V |2 |F(λ)|∥λ∥) iterations, where ∥λ∥ := maxX∈S(V ) λ(X). By
using the Red-win strategy in Theorem 1, we can conduct the uncrossing procedure in time
polynomial in |V | and |F(λ)|, not depending on the bit length of λ. Thus we have:
Theorem 2. Any λ : S(V ) → R+ can be uncrossed in time polynomial of |V | and |F(λ)|.
    This is a natural extension of [5, Theorem 2] to general skew-supermodular functions.
In [2, Section 3.3], we found that the Red-win strategy of the uncrossing game also brings
an interesting optimality property of laminar solutions, where we proved this property for
{0, 1}-valued skew-supermodular functions (as a consequence of Karzanov’s uncrossing al-
gorithm). Now we can state and prove this for general skew-supermodular functions.
Proposition 3. Let λ be a nonoptimal laminar feasible function. For any ϵ > 0, there
exists a laminar feasible solution λ∗ such that ∥λ − λ∗ ∥ ≤ ϵ and the objective value of λ∗ is
greater than that of λ.

λ and an optimal solution. Apply the uncrossing procedure to λ′ according to the strategy
of Theorem 1. Then we obtain a laminar feasible solution λ∗ with the objective value not
less than that of λ′ .

the laminarity requirement. The set of all laminar feasible solutions is not convex in the
space of all functions on S(V ). Nevertheless this proposition says that the objective be-

laminarity-preserving primal-dual algorithm, like Edmonds’ blossom algorithm, for general
